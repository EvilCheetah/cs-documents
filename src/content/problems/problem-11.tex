\item[\textbf{Problem 11}]
Alice designs a double-RSA cipher. She first generates two secret primes $p$ and
$q$, and compute $n = p * q$, then choose two public encryption exponents $e_1$
and $e_2$ that are relatively prime to $\phi(n)$. So $\bvec{e_1, e_2, n}$
becomes the public key. She tells people to encrypt message $M$ by computing
$C_1 = M^{e_1} \bmod n$ and then $C = C_1^{e_2} \bmod n$, finally sending just
$C$ to her.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[a.] Show the decryption process (i.e., how Alice can obtain the
    plaintext $M$ from the final ciphertext $C$).

    \item[b.] Is the double-RSA cipher more secure, less secure, or just as
    secure as the regular RSA cipher with the same modulus $n$ but only one
    encryption exponent? Why?

    \item[c.] Charlie got Alice's instructions confused, and encrypt message $M$
    for Alice using $e_1$ and $e_2$ in the reverse order (i.e., Charlie uses
    $C_1 = M^{e_2} \bmod n$ then $C = C_1^{e_1} \bmod n$). What would happen
    when Alice, unaware of Charlie's error, tries to decipher the ciphertext
    using her usual procedure?
\end{enumerate}

\Solution

\subsection{Subproblem A}

In order to decrypt the message from the ciphertext, Alice first needs to
calculate $n$ and $\phi(n)$ using her secret primes $p$ and $q$:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
    n       &= p \cdot q          \\
    \phi(n) &= (p - 1)(q - 1)
\end{alignat*}

The final ciphertext $C$ encrypted using the public exponents $e_1$ and $e_2$
expands as follows:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
    C       &= C_{1}^{e_2}         \bmod n \\
            &= (M^{e_{1}})^{e_{2}} \bmod n \\
            &= M^{e_{1}e_{2}}      \bmod n
\end{alignat*}

Since both $e_1$ and $e_2$ are public, we can represent their product as a
single public exponent $e = e_1 e_2$. Because Alice knows $\phi(n)$, she can
calculate the corresponding private decryption exponent $d$:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
    d       &= e^{-1}              \bmod \phi(n) \\
            &= (e_1 e_2)^{-1}      \bmod \phi(n)
\end{alignat*}

Based on the properties of RSA, we know that $e \cdot d \equiv 1
\pmod{\phi(n)}$. Therefore, Alice can obtain the plaintext $M$ from the incoming
ciphertext $C$ using the standard RSA decryption procedure:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
    M       &=  C^{d}              \bmod n \\
            &= (M^{e})^{d}         \bmod n \\
            &= M^{ed}              \bmod n \\
            &= M^{1}               \bmod n \\
            &= M
\end{alignat*}


\subsection{Subproblem B}

The double-RSA cipher is just as secure as a regular RSA cipher with the same
modulus $n$. It is neither more nor less secure. Because mathematically, the
``double'' encryption collapses into a single standard RSA encryption. By
computing $(M^{e_1})^{e_2} \bmod n$, you are simply performing a standard RSA
encryption using a new public exponent where
\[
    e = e_1 \cdot e_2
\]

The security of RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring the modulus $n$. Since
$n$ remains exactly the same, the fundamental mathematical hardness of the
problem has not changed. An attacker doesn't need to break two layers of RSA;
they just need to break one layer of RSA that happens to use a slightly larger
public exponent.


\subsection{Subproblem C}

If Charlie encrypts in the reverse order, he first uses $e_2$ and then $e_1$.
His math looks like this:

\[
    C_{Charlie} = (M^{e_2})^{e_1} \bmod n = M^{e_2 \cdot e_1} \bmod n
\]

Because basic multiplication is commutative, Charlie's resulting ciphertext is
exactly identical to the ciphertext he would have generated if he had followed
the instructions correctly.

Therefore, when Alice tries to decipher it using her usual procedure, she will
successfully recover the plaintext $M$ without ever realizing Charlie made an
error.
